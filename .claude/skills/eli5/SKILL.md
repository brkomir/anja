# /eli5 — the through-line, in plain English

Answers *"what is actually going on, and what do I need to decide?"* for an owner who has
been away from the detail, or in it so long the thread has frayed. It exists so the owner does
not lose track of the owner's own thoughts: rebuilding THE OWNER'S thread is the job,
and plain English is how.

**Every reply opens with `[eli5]`**, and nothing comes before it — no progress line while the derivation runs (6 of 47 replies after the 2026-10-15 fold opened on one; one of them came back an hour later as a request to explain its 3 items again). **Read-only:** no commits, no
mail, no tracker or memory edits, no file writes. If something needs filing, say so and do it
as a separate, named act.

## Before you send — check the draft against these 11

Items 1-4, 10 and 11 answer problems found in earlier outputs; 5, 6, 7 and 9 are an expert panel's
reading of past outputs (6 and 9 were shown to the owner, 5 only as lead-ins inside, 7 not at all), adopted together with check 4's
threshold, check 8 and the example below. This file is what loads at the moment you write, which
the memory files holding them are not (measured 2026-10-14 over 355 outputs of this command:
100% wrote counts as words, 96% carried a paragraph over 3 sentences, headings fell from 47% of
September's outputs to 11% of October's).

1. **Every fact was derived this turn, or its own sentence says it was not** — *"I haven't
   checked whether…"*, *"that came from another session"*, *"this is from memory"*. Never in a footer.
2. **Numbers are digits:** 6 experts, 12 languages, 2 commits, $3, 0.7 cents, 10 minutes, 7 of
   the 8. A count at the start of a sentence is a digit too (*2 limits on that:*, *2 corrections*), and so is one that names a choice (*close at 2*). No size threshold and no exceptions — digits are allowed for every number, and typing
   numbers out does not add to readability. Keep every number the work earned, inside a sentence, and a comparison (steadier, cheaper, beat, missed more) carries its numbers in the sentence that makes it (a bare *"X was steadier"* left the owner asking for the numbers).
   "One" as a pronoun (*one of them*) is not a count.
3. **Open with the answer, alone** — after the quoted block when you are quoting the owner (check 10);
   1 or 2 sentences, no label on it. A bare `/eli5` may open on 1 line saying what follows, as
   the example does.
4. **A `##` heading per subject once the reply passes about 200 words**, in the praised
   message's words where they fit (*What we set out to do* · *What happened* · *What's waiting
   on you*); under that, no headings. A part with nothing in it is left out, never printed empty.
   This is a trial the owner can end the moment the owner complains about headings.
5. **A new subject opens with a bold lead-in that says what happened** (*"**You wanted a summary
   box at the top of each lesson.**"*, *"**Then a naming problem.**"*), never a bare label like *"**Naming.**"*. A paragraph continuing the one
   above takes none. Bold lives only there, on list-item names (check 7), on card labels and on the closing labels; no
   mid-sentence bold, no emoji, no warning glyphs.
6. **At most 80 words per paragraph**: a sentence that would carry it past 80 starts the next
   paragraph. A sentence over 40 words is split. (Measured 2026-10-14: the praised message's
   longest paragraph is 75 words, its longest sentence 38, and its sentences average 13; recent
   replies ran 150 to 230 words a paragraph. A
   sentence count is the wrong unit — that message's first story paragraph is 5 sentences.)
7. **3 or more parallel things of 1 or 2 sentences each are a list, 1 per line** —
   `- **Name** — 1 or 2 sentences.` The parts of a board, the items packed into a quoted line,
   things checked (a decision's choices follow check 11). A parallel thing that needs a paragraph
   keeps its bold lead-in (check 5), as the 4 events in the example below do. Numbered only
   when order matters (steps the owner performs, a ranking). No table unless the owner asked for one: the owner's pane
   has clipped one wide table (2026-09-23) and stacked another into cards (2026-10-14).
8. **Current state with 3 or more facts the owner would re-check later** (committed, pushed, running,
   waiting on review) **may be a card**: up to 6 lines of `- **Label:** value`, plain-word
   labels, each value a number, name or state with its age — never a *because*, which goes in a
   sentence under the card. It is a shape that has read well on the fleet board,
   and the one shape the praised message does not
   contain, so it is optional: drop it the first time the owner calls one a summary. It and the closing labels
   (check 11) are the only places a number sits outside a sentence; the label keeps it legible.
9. **Every coined work-word gets its plain meaning the first time it appears, in that same
   sentence** — fixture, smoke, the bar, the series, the fold, the pause record. The owner is reading
   this because the owner lost the thread, so a word the owner used an hour ago is one the owner no longer holds. On
   2026-10-14 `/eli5` was run on 2 paragraphs of one `/eli5` answer within 2 minutes; the sentence
   that failed read *"the ledger holds exactly one fx-04 row, the seven-tenths-of-a-cent smoke
   from this afternoon"*. Product nouns — the product's own names for what it sells and does — are the owner's words. A closing item gets read apart from the body — pasted back alone, re-read after a compaction, or on a phone — so a work-word in an item is said in plain words there too, even when the body already defined it. The example has no such item, so this is optional: drop it on its Revisit trigger.
10. **Quoted text sits in a block before it is explained** — the owner's line or a row in a `>` block;
    a UI string, a config value or anything the owner will copy in a fenced block: what it says now,
    what you propose, how it renders.
11. **It ends on what is the owner's, counted, and nothing follows it** but an optional "That's it."
    (and "Everything else is done and checked." only if you checked it this turn) — under its own
    heading when the reply has headings (check 4), in its own paragraphs otherwise. Each decision
    is a numbered question the owner can answer in a word: 1 plain sentence on what it is about, then the
    question with its choices, the way the praised message does it (*"an already-published lesson
    describes that same deleted screen. Same fix, or leave it?"*), then your recommendation in 1
    clause (the praised message has none; the preferred version attaches it to the
    first option). 3 or more choices go under the question, 1 per line. When 2 of the choices can both be done, say so, or offer the pair as a choice (a fork written as either-or once drew the owner's question why not do all of them; not in the example, so optional on its Revisit trigger). Never a label followed by choices chained
    with commas — *"Two words from you unlock the chain: the shape, fx-04 or the other fixture,
    and the extension, yes with up to three dollars, decide at the trigger, or no"* left the owner
    asking what exactly there was to rule on, and *"the sentence stays, goes, or
    grows"* left the owner still not knowing what to decide.
    The split is do-vs-decide, both sides the owner's. What you will do next, if anything, is 1 clause
    inside the last item; an empty side is left out, not announced. Anything else this turn owes the owner (a mail, a listener re-arm, a peer's verdict) is done BEFORE the `[eli5]` reply, with no line of it above the marker, or waits for the owner's next message: the closing ask is the last thing on the owner's screen.

**No apparatus in sentences.** Paths, shas, symbols, rule names and row ids are things the owner would
use to check you, not to act. A name the owner needs to DO the next thing stays (a command, a migration
number); a coined name the owner cannot resolve becomes plain words (check 9).

## ⛔ The Iron rule — DERIVE, and where you did not, say so IN the sentence

The plain register strips paths, shas and rule names because they are noise to the owner, and they are
also what would let the owner check a claim. So an eli5 is where a wrong claim is least likely to be
caught and most likely to be acted on: **plain, confident and wrong is this command's failure, not length.**

Before a sentence goes in, ask whether something you ran this turn produced it. Read each number
out of the artifact — a results file, a test run, a tracker row, git — never out of context. A
number you cannot re-derive cheaply does not go in.

On the day this command was created, 3 plain-English claims were wrong — a diffraction limit, a
"bright daylight" reading off an ISO value, and "that measurement does not exist" — all recalled
rather than derived. 2 of them were caught by a human reader; nothing else would have caught them.

## Process

### Bare `/eli5` — the current situation

1. **Derive the live state:** `git fetch origin`, the unpushed count (say it is fleet-wide, not
   yours), `git status --porcelain`, and the artifact the active arc actually turns on.
2. **Find the through-line.** What is the owner trying to get to, and what single thing stands between
   the owner and it? 3 co-equal threads means it has not been thought through yet.
3. **Tell it as a story with the owner in it:** what we set out to do · what happened · what is in the
   way · what is waiting on the owner. Someone does something in each sentence, in past tense.
4. **Name a reversal only if the owner could have acted on the wrong version.** Then 3 sentences: what
   you said, what was wrong, what is true — never how you found it. If the wrong version never
   reached a decision of the owner's, fix it silently.
5. **End on what is the owner's** (check 11).

### `/eli5 "<a line of mine>"` — what the owner mostly types

In October 2026, 67 of the owner's 69 typed `/eli5` calls carried a quoted passage (300 of the owner's 360
typed calls all-time). By the
split between the 2 commands that is `/explain`'s anchor, and the owner uses this command for it anyway: do not
bounce the owner to the other one.

1. **Quote the line first, in a `>` block**, then find where you wrote it and read the whole
   paragraph or row around it — a pasted cell is a fragment of a claim.
2. **Run `/explain`'s 4 checks on it before explaining it** — still true, not closed by a peer,
   really the owner's, the size I gave it right. If a check fails, the first sentence's answer to the owner's question starts from that (*"That line is
   out of date: …"*).
3. **Read the words after the quotation: they are the owner's question, and the first sentence answers
   them** — then what the line means, then each coined word in it, in the order it appears,
   bringing in no new coined words while explaining it.
4. **Stay read-only.** If the line sits in a tracker row that is now wrong, say so; fixing the row
   is `/explain`'s write, and you can offer it in 1 clause.
5. If the quote carries an ask, end with it as numbered questions (check 11).

### `/eli5 <topic>` — one thing, plainly

1. **Open every surface that currently claims something about it, this turn** — the code, a
   tracker row, a `DECISIONS.md` entry, an active plan — never a memory of them.
2. **When they disagree, the disagreement is the answer.** Say which surface says what and which
   one is load-bearing. One confident paragraph over 4 disagreeing sources reads exactly like a good answer.
3. **If the topic is a piece of text, show it in a block first** (check 10).
4. Explain the mechanism in ordinary words, with its real numbers.
5. Say what you did not check, and what would settle it.

## What a good one looks like

Match this; do not audit against it. It is the bare-form message with which, for the owner, the
full context landed for the first time, nearly verbatim: its 10 spelled-out counts are digits (check 2), and the receipts line is dropped. That line said every fact in the
message had been checked in the turns before it; that now goes inside a sentence, and a fact you
did not derive this turn says so where it sits (check 1). Its opening "Sure." is a courtesy and
goes; the line after it is the 1-line opener check 3 allows a bare `/eli5`. A line or topic answer is shorter and has
no "what happened" arc.

> Sure. Here's the whole thing without the jargon.
>
> ## What we set out to do
>
> Gather every outstanding piece of work on the `/learn` pages and make a plan. Then we started actually doing it.
>
> ## What happened
>
> **You wanted a summary box at the top of each lesson.** 6 experts looked at it. All 6 said don't. 2 reasons: the page already opens with 3 layers of summary before any teaching, and — the interesting one — a short version of a lesson can teach the *opposite* of the lesson. On the clutter page, the most quotable line says "remove distractions," while the lesson says twice that it isn't about removing things.
>
> What they said instead: people can't *find* anything on these pages. The section headings are the best map in the product and they're invisible, spread over 1,700 words with nothing to click. So we built a row of jump links. That's live.
>
> **While reviewing that, someone found a real bug nobody was looking for.** If you tabbed backwards onto one of those links, it parked itself behind the sticky bar at the top — completely hidden. Fixed.
>
> **Then a naming problem.** There are 2 menus on a lesson page: the bar at the top that takes you to other pages, and the new strip that jumps around the current page. For blind users they were announced with nearly the same name — "Learn sections" and "Lesson sections." In Polish they sounded almost identical.
>
> The panel split on which one to rename. Then the naming rule that everything on `/learn` is called "lessons," settled it: my new strip was named correctly, and the *old top bar* was the one lying — it takes you to other pages while calling itself "sections." Renamed it in 12 languages.
>
> **And we wrote the new lesson** — the one about capture ceilings, where something about the shot itself limits the score no matter how you edit it. 4 experts reviewed it and found 4 places where it described the product wrongly. The worst: it told readers to look for a score on screen that was deleted back on the 25th. The author fixed all of it.
>
> ## What's waiting on you
>
> **1 thing to do:** read the lesson draft. Nothing can go live or get translated until you sign it off.
>
> **1 thing to decide:** an already-published lesson describes that same deleted screen. Same fix, or leave it?
>
> That's it. Everything else is done and checked.

Why it works, measured: 12 numbers in its 414 words as shown, all digits, and 0 file paths (the
original's "18 in 445" counted 5 pronoun "one"s, "twice", and the receipts line's 31 words); the original ran 2.9x
longer than the message it replaced, so plain never means shorter or fewer facts; its longest paragraph is 75 words and its
sentences average 13; every subject is a person or a thing that acted; it ends on what the owner owes,
in the owner's words.

## Boundaries

- **Not `/summary`**, which is an instrument-shaped state readout the owner scans; this is a narrative
  the owner reads. A list of parallel things and a state card (checks 7 and 8) are not tables: they hold
  what a table would have held, in a form the owner's pane shows the same way at any width.
  **Not `/explain`**, which audits a claim of mine and fixes the row it lives in.
- **Do not soften a bad state** to make a cleaner story. "2 unrelated things are broken" is a valid eli5.
- **Do not re-litigate.** If the owner has ruled, the ruling is the state, not an open question.
- **Not a licence elsewhere.** Every owner-facing message is already written this way
  (`.claude/orchestrator/conventions.md` § Write to the owner the way you would say it out loud);
  this command produces 1 specific artifact.
- **A comparison across arms with numbers (models, efforts, versions, cost, time) is /report's.**
  Answer the owner's first question in 1 sentence, then build the page or offer it in 1 clause.

## Revisit

- 3 consecutive runs draw a factual correction from the owner ⇒ the Iron rule is not being applied; the
  fix is derivation discipline, not more prose rules.
- The owner stops invoking it because the standing register lands on its own ⇒ success; retire it.
- It answers with tables, or with sections named like a readout (status, needs you, plan) rather
  than the parts of a story ⇒ it has collapsed into `/summary`. Headings alone are not the drift:
  the praised message has 3.
- The owner complains about headings ⇒ the revert is bold lead-ins only (check 4 goes).
- The owner calls a state card "a summary" or asks for the story instead ⇒ check 8 goes.
- Before adding a check above, find it in the example; a check the example does not contain is
  optional with its own removal trigger, as check 8 is. This spec once added 5 sections the praised
  message lacked, and the next message the owner rejected carried all 5.
